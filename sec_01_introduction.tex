\chapter{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

\section{Motivation} \label{sec:introduction-motivation}

Many of today’s scientific and machine learning models---including billion-dollar recommendation models and large-scale scientific solvers and simulators---are inherently sparse~\cite{gupta2020hpca,gupta2020isca,williams2007sc}. Yet modern datacenters and supercomputers are optimized for dense computation~\cite{nguyen2022ccpe}. This fundamental mismatch calls for architectures and systems that can execute sparse workloads efficiently.

A tensor---a multidimensional array---is said to be \emph{sparse} when most of its entries are zero~\cite{kjolstad2017oopsla}. Many real-world datasets naturally exhibit that structure~\cite{davis2011university}. For example, the \textit{follow} relation in a social network can be represented as a matrix $A$ where $A_{i,j} = 1$ if user $i$ follows user $j$, and zero otherwise~\cite{leskovec2014snap}. Since users typically follow only a small fraction of all other users, this matrix is overwhelmingly sparse. Similarly, the internal states of scientific solvers and simulations often contain mostly zero-valued elements~\cite{barrett1994book,williams2007sc}.

Because zero elements neither need to be stored nor computed, sparse tensors are represented using compressed formats that track only nonzero values and their coordinates~\cite{barrett1994book}. Operating on these formats, however, requires irregular traversals and data-dependent control flow~\cite{sgherzi2024jpdc}. For example, summation requires merging nonzeros based on their coordinates, which entails frequent data-dependent branch operations~\cite{ahrens2023cgo}. Multiplication often requires locating tensor fibers that share a common index, leading to both irregular memory accesses and additional data-dependent control flow.

Traditional datacenters and supercomputers are not designed for such irregular computation. Instead, they are heavily optimized for dense and regular workloads---such as dense matrix multiplication and convolution---where data is laid out contiguously and accessed with predictable patterns~\cite{wilkinson2022pmbs,nassif2022isscc,nvidia2022hopper}. On these architectures, however, data-dependent branches cause frequent misspeculation and costly pipeline flushes~\cite{sgherzi2024jpdc}. Meanwhile, irregular memory accesses degrade cache utilization and increase memory latency, ultimately clogging the memory subsystem~\cite{wulf1995hitting,sgherzi2024jpdc}.

As sparse workloads become increasingly pervasive, there is growing interest in hardware and software solutions tailored to their unique characteristics~\cite{gupta2020hpca}. Programmable prefetchers represent an initial attempt to adapt traditional general-purpose architectures to irregular access patterns~\cite{yu2015micro}. Although they require minimal hardware changes, they can only accelerate irregular memory accesses, not control flow, and provide limited speedups~\cite{siracusa2023micro}. At the other extreme, specialized accelerators for fixed sparse kernels can deliver substantial performance gains~\cite{zhang2021asplos}, but at the cost of limited flexibility. Reconfigurable architectures broaden the range of supported kernels~\cite{rucker2021micro,weng2020isca}, yet still suffer from classic accelerator drawbacks, including kernel launch overheads and constrained memory capacity.

Decoupled Access--Execute (DAE) architectures~\cite{smith1982isca} offer a promising middle ground between the flexibility of general-purpose processors and the efficiency of specialization. They separate memory access from computation into distinct units, typically connected through queues: an access unit optimized for memory operations, and an execute unit---often a general-purpose core---focused on computation. Hyperscalers have already demonstrated the potential of this design by accelerating irregular vector lookups in recommendation models~\cite{jouppi2023isca,firoozshahian2023isca}, while other authors have shown similar benefits for scalar lookup operations in scientific computing~\cite{barredo2021sc,orenesvera2022isca}. Prior work has also demonstrated that DAE designs can successfully optimize more complex sparse operations, including sparse-format compression and decompression~\cite{kanellopoulos2019micro,yang2021isca} and irregular sparse merging~\cite{scheffler2021indirection,scheffler2023sparse}. However, although current proposals demonstrate the promise of DAE designs, they do not yet realize the full potential of this approach: they either target narrow classes of sparse tensor algebra operations, do not fully leverage modern vector compute cores, or leave much of the programmability burden to the user. This thesis addresses these limitations by proposing a vector-friendly DAE architecture and compiler stack specifically designed to accelerate sparse tensor algebra operations.

\section{Objectives} \label{sec:introduction-objectives}

The primary goal of this thesis is to overcome the fundamental challenges of general sparse tensor algebra and enable its efficient execution at scale. To achieve this goal, we propose a hardware--software co-design based on a DAE architecture that spans both processor microarchitecture and the compiler stack. Unlike other solutions in the literature, we aim to design a DAE system that can efficiently accelerate a broad range of sparse and dense tensor algebra operations. We identify the following specific research objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Characterize the microarchitectural implications of sparsity:} To design efficient hardware, we must first quantify how irregular control flow and memory access impact the performance of sparse tensor operations in scientific and machine learning workloads. Although it is well known that sparse kernels such as sparse matrix-vector multiplication perform poorly on traditional architectures~\cite{lin2010design,bell2009implementing,sadi2019micro,fowers2014fccm,alappat2022ccpe,grigoras2015fccm,umuroglu2014energy,williams2007sc,nurvitadhi2015sparse}, our objective is to generalize this understanding to a broader class of sparse tensor algebra operations in different domains, each with distinct characteristics and performance challenges, and to delve deeper into the fundamental causes of their inefficiency. By characterizing how these workloads stress modern processors, we establish a detailed understanding of why existing architectures fall short and what requirements future designs must satisfy to scale effectively.

    \item \textbf{Design an efficient vector-friendly DAE architecture for sparse tensor algebra:} Building on this analysis, we aim to develop a DAE architecture capable of overcoming all the identified bottlenecks. Our objective is to design a general and scalable solution that accelerates a broad class of sparse tensor algebra workloads while preserving the throughput of modern vector execution and outperforming traditional CPUs and GPUs in both raw performance and performance per watt.

    \item \textbf{Hide DAE programming complexity from end users:} To facilitate widespread adoption of the proposed architecture, we aim to hide the complexity of the DAE programming model from end users. In scientific computing, this complexity can often be hidden behind small hand-written libraries, such as DAE implementations of BLAS-like routines. This approach, however, does not scale to machine learning models. Modern machine learning compilers explore a wide range of formats, precisions, optimizations, and fusion opportunities, making it impractical to hand-write DAE kernels for every variant. Therefore, to fully unlock the benefits of the proposed architecture, we aim to design a generic compiler stack that lowers sparse machine learning operations to DAE code while integrating with frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow~\cite{paszke2019neurips,abadi2015tensorflow}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions} \label{sec:introduction-contributions}

This thesis addresses these research objectives through the following contributions.

\subsection{Performance Characterization of Sparse Workloads} \label{sec:introduction-performance-characterization}

The first contribution of this thesis is a comprehensive analysis of sparse tensor algebra operations across scientific and machine learning workloads. In \Cref{sec:architectural-implications}, we show that irregular tensor traversal and merging are fundamental bottlenecks in traditional architectures. We first benchmark off-the-shelf CPUs and GPUs and demonstrate that irregular memory accesses and control flow can stall computation in several sparse tensor algebra workloads for up to 99\% of the time. We then characterize representative algorithms and inputs, demonstrating how characteristics such as spatio-temporal locality relate to these bottlenecks. Finally, we model the scaling of performance, power, and area to demonstrate that scaling up and out traditional compute cores is not a viable path toward efficient sparse tensor algebra.

This contribution led to the following publications:

\subsection{The Tensor Marshaling Unit} \label{sec:introduction-tmu}

The second contribution is the \emph{Tensor Marshaling Unit} (TMU), a specialized hardware unit that offloads and decouples tensor traversal and merging from the compute core, enabling scalable execution. Unlike prior DAE designs, the TMU (1)~supports a broad class of sparse and dense tensor algebra expressions, and (2)~integrates seamlessly with modern vector units to exploit structured and unstructured parallelism. In \Cref{sec:tmu}, we present the TMU architecture, its integration into a multicore DAE processor composed of TMUs and CPUs, and a detailed evaluation of performance, power, and area. The resulting DAE processor outperforms traditional CPUs by up to an order of magnitude and GPUs by up to 6$\times$ in both performance and performance per watt.

\subsection{The Ember Compiler} \label{sec:introduction-ember}

The third contribution is the \emph{Ember compiler}, a generic compiler that lowers sparse machine learning operations to optimized DAE code. Ember is implemented in MLIR, enabling integration with PyTorch through torch-mlir and with TensorFlow through its native MLIR compiler stack. In \Cref{sec:dae-compilation}, we first show why manually writing and optimizing DAE programs for machine learning workloads is challenging. We then present Ember’s intermediate representations and compilation strategies, which are designed for automated DAE optimization and integrate tightly with modern tensor compilers. Finally, we demonstrate that Ember achieves performance comparable to hand-optimized implementations, unlocking the TMU’s potential without imposing additional programmability burden on users.
